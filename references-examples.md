# Robotic Prototyping helper guidebooks

*Facts and links checked 2026-10-22.*

* [Game Manual 0](https://gm0.org/en/latest/): helps a new team decide what to build on. The
  community guide for FIRST Tech Challenge teams (on GitHub, CC BY-NC 2.0, last updated 2026-10)
  has a kit guide that sets the build systems (goBILDA, REV, TETRIX, the discontinued Actobotics)
  against going custom, a chapter on custom manufacturing (3D printing, machining, laser cutting),
  and a [design strategy](https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/design-skills/design-strategy.html) page
  of one-line rules: "A good execution of a bad design will beat a bad execution of good design",
  "Build for the worst case scenario, not the best case scenario". Our Parts step opens with the
  same choice (kit, open design or from scratch), and rules that short are what our *Traps* could
  sound like.
* [FRCDesign.org learning course](https://www.frcdesign.org/learning-course/): teaches CAD by having
  you design real robot mechanisms, with less help each time. The Onshape course for FIRST Robotics
  Competition teams aims to "take you from zero to being able to model a full robot": stage 1 is
  fundamentals with numbered exercises, stage 2 is four mechanism projects (shooter, pivot, intake,
  elevator) that follow the same pattern, from engineering concepts to layout sketch to assembly,
  and "gradually get less guided". Our tracks could fade the same way, holding the reader's hand in
  the first steps and leaving more to them in the later ones.
* [Fab Academy: project management](https://academy.cba.mit.edu/classes/project_management/index.html):
  names the habits that get a project finished. The class page lists the principles Fab Academy
  students work by: spiral development, demand- versus supply-side time management, triage, serial
  versus parallel development, bottom-up versus top-down debugging, modularity, and "document,
  clean as you work". Spiral development (get a rough version of the whole thing working, then
  improve the whole) is the idea behind our one-demo rule, and supply-side time management (plan
  to the time you have) is why step 1 sets a date. Using these names would give the Plan step
  words that makers already know.
* [THINK. MAKE. START.](https://www.tms.tum.de/): gets a team from a problem to a demo in two
  weeks. In TUM's prototyping course (6 ECTS, run with UnternehmerTUM), 50 students from all
  disciplines "Build innovative products in just 2 weeks"; each team has a Problem Expert, a Tech
  Expert and a Business Expert, and every batch ends at a public Demo Day (the next in March 2027).
  It is our six steps pressed into two weeks with a fixed public finish line, a model for a Robonn
  build sprint in Year 2, and the three roles would help teams that form in our Discord.
* [ETH Focus Projects](https://www.designsociety.org/publication/35950/ETH+Focus+Projects+%E2%80%93+Successful+Approaches+for+Project-Based+Education+in+Engineering+Design):
  gives student teams freedom and still gets them to a tested prototype. In the third year of ETH
  Zurich's mechanical engineering bachelor, teams of 5 to 10 students from mechanical and
  electrical engineering and industrial design take a product "from market profile to tested
  prototype". This 2014 paper by the course team names what makes it work: extensive testing for
  factual feedback, coaching built on values (responsibility, transparency, open feedback), and a
  question-driven stage-gate process that "gives the student teams the freedom to experiment and
  still enough orientation". Our *Done when* lines are stage gates already; written as questions,
  with the values named, they would come closer to this.
* [Stanford Biodesign Guide](https://biodesignguide.stanford.edu/): keeps what matters at every
  step in one place. Stanford's toolkits for student teams in health technology come in three
  parts: *Identify* (finding a need), *Invent* (solutions, IP, regulation, business) and *At Every
  Step* (teamwork, ethical decisions, inclusion, presenting). Prototyping alone has five toolkits,
  Question, Plan, Build, Test, and Assess and Iterate, the first on "how to take a question-driven
  approach to prototyping". The order is nearly our six steps. What we lack is the *At Every Step*
  layer: safety, teamwork, documentation and values turn up here and there in our steps (the LiPo
  note in Build, insurance on the drone track) rather than as one thread through all of them. The
  toolkits sit behind a sign-up.
* [iFixit's guide details](https://www.ifixit.com/Help/Guide_Details): tells you what a job takes
  before you start. Every iFixit repair guide states its difficulty and a time estimate, which
  authors are told to keep generous because first-timers are slower, and it keeps two lists:
  tools, which you keep, and parts, "items that you probably will no longer have afterwards". Our
  steps already give time and cost. Splitting each track's budget into tools (bought once, or used
  at the MakerSpace) and parts (bought for every build) would make the cost line more honest.
* [507 Mechanical Movements](https://507movements.com/): shows mechanisms moving, so you can pick
  one. An 1868 catalogue of mechanisms, from gearing to steam engines and clockwork, is online with
  animations for many of its drawings, and more are being added. Cornell's
  [KMODDL](https://engineering.library.cornell.edu/kmoddl) does the same for its collection of
  19th-century kinematic models. For the arm, legged and field tracks, a page of mechanisms to
  choose from, each one moving, would help more than a list of names.
* [L'Atelier Paysan](https://www.latelierpaysan.org/Outils-et-plans): lets farmers build their own
  machines. The French cooperative published about 123 construction plans and 12 technical guides
  for farm tools, from market-garden hoes and mobile hen houses to vineyard tool bars and
  animal-traction sleds, under CC BY-NC-SA, and ran training in which farmers build them, in the
  name of technological sovereignty. A commercial court [ordered its liquidation](https://www.latelierpaysan.org/)
  on 2026-04-21, and the site now carries an appeal to keep its knowledge from commercial
  appropriation. It is the clearest model for agricultural builds made with the people who use
  them, and a reminder that open plans need an organisation that can pay its way.
* [Crowd Supply's guide](https://www.crowdsupply.com/guide): walks a creator from a working
  prototype to a shipped product. The crowdfunding platform's handbook goes in the order things
  happen: when to submit, the life cycle of a campaign and why projects are rejected, then pricing,
  working with manufacturers and CE certification before launch, and fulfilment after it. It is
  where our *after* step points once a build becomes a product, and its page on why projects are
  rejected is the same kind of list as our Traps.

## Helper Guidelines for High-Tech Robotics Builders

* [Technology Readiness Levels](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/somd/space-operations/technology-readiness-levels/):
  tells a builder how mature a technology is, in words funders use. NASA's nine-level scale runs
  from basic research (TRL 1) to "flight proven" (TRL 9), and research funders such as the EU use
  it too. At TRL 3 a proof-of-concept model is built, at TRL 4 components are tested together, and
  at TRL 6 there is a fully functional prototype. A credible first build sits around TRL 3 to 4;
  saying so on the *after* step would tell students how far they have come.
* [Kickstarter's rules for hardware](https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16236428-what-are-the-rules-for-hardware-and-product-design-projects):
  draws the line between a prototype and a promise. Hardware creators are "required to show
  working prototypes", "We do not allow photorealistic renderings", and creators must say how they
  will produce the product and whether they have made anything like it before. The rule goes back
  to 2012, when Kickstarter told backers it is not a store. It is an outside test of what
  *credible* means, and it matches the one-take demo our Plan and Show steps ask for.
* [NIST test methods for response robots](https://www.nist.gov/el/intelligent-systems-division-73500/response-robots):
  makes test results comparable across teams and places. NIST's standard tests for ground robots,
  drones and boats cover mobility, manipulation, sensors, energy, communications, operator
  proficiency, logistics and safety, and are standardised through the ASTM committee E54.09. The
  apparatus is meant to be cheap: one of the standards (E3408) says it is "low cost and easy to
  fabricate so they can be widely replicated", and the drone lanes are two-gallon buckets with
  printed stickers, one open lane serving five tests. Our Test step counts five clean runs, but
  each team in its own room. A test lane anyone can build in a seminar room, published with its
  score sheet, would make builds comparable.
* [WHO Surgical Safety Checklist](https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/patient-safety/research/safe-surgery/tool-and-resources):
  makes a team stop and check before the risky moment. Its 19 items are checked at three pauses:
  before anaesthesia, before the first incision, and before the patient leaves the operating room.
  The team must "verbally confirm each item", "Adaptation after local consultation is encouraged",
  and most surgical providers in the world now use it. Our Test step records every run but has no
  check before one. A short list for robots, read aloud before power-on, before the first motion
  and before packing up (stop button within reach, battery checked, area clear, camera running),
  would be its equivalent.
* [Duckietown city specification](https://docs.duckietown.com/ente/duckietown-manual/30-duckietown-city/appearance-specifications/duckietown-smart-city-tile-map.html):
  makes a test environment that anyone can rebuild. Duckietown's town for its small self-driving
  robots is built to rules: roads are 61 × 61 cm foam tiles of five kinds (straight, curve,
  three-way, four-way, empty), with a solid 4.8 cm white line on the right, a dashed yellow centre
  line and red stop lines at intersections only, and further layers cover
  [traffic signs](https://docs.duckietown.com/ente/duckietown-manual/30-duckietown-city/appearance-specifications/duckietown-smart-city-traffic-signs-layer.html)
  (each with an AprilTag, centred 13 cm above the floor), traffic lights and parking. It is a city
  guideline written for robots, and the model for a portable test town for the mobile track, built
  to the same rules in any seminar room so that runs can be compared.
* [DIN SPEC 3105](https://www.din.de/de/wdc-beuth:din21:324805763): says what open hardware
  documentation must contain. Germany's standard for open source hardware is free to download;
  part 1 (July 2020) turns the open hardware definition into checkable requirements for technical
  documentation, and part 2 covers how conformity is assessed. It is developed in the open on
  [OSEG's GitLab](https://gitlab.com/OSEGermany/OHS-3105) under CC BY-SA 4.0, as "Requirements for
  technical documentation and community-based assessment". The directory has OSHWA's definition
  and certification; this adds a German checklist, and its community-based assessment suggests a
  club version: a build counts as documented once another member could rebuild it from the
  repository.
* [GitBuilding](https://gitbuilding.io/): turns build notes into assembly instructions and a parts
  list. It is "an open source program for writing hardware assembly instructions": Markdown with a
  small extra syntax, parts tagged where the text mentions them, a bill of materials generated per
  variant, and output as HTML, PDF or Markdown with the BOM as CSV. It warns that the syntax may
  still change. It works the way our site does, plain text in git with a strict syntax and
  generated pages, which makes it the tool to recommend for the Show step's bar, "Someone else
  could rebuild it".
* [SS 713:2025](https://www.singaporestandardseshop.sg/Product/SSPdtDetail/4bc72ff7-951f-4a5e-b85f-edafe36a8a3d)
  and [NAVER 1784](https://www.navercorp.com/en/service/1784): tell robot builders how to use lifts
  and doors. Singapore's standard (replacing TR 93:2021) sets the minimum data exchanges, hardware
  and safety requirements for robots that ride lifts and pass automatic doors, including during
  fire evacuation and power cuts. NAVER's second headquarters was built so that its service robots
  can move around freely, with ROBOPORT, "the world's first robot-only elevator". A mobile robot
  that leaves its room meets doors and lifts first, and when Robonn looks for its Year 2 space,
  step-free floors, wide doors and a lift a robot can call are easier to choose than to add.

## City Guidelines for Builders & Entrepreneurs

* [The New Essential Guide to Electronics in Shenzhen](https://www.crowdsupply.com/machinery-enchantress/the-new-essential-guide-to-electronics-in-shenzhen):
  gets a builder through a city's electronics markets without the language. The printed guide was
  first made in 2016 as "a lean, practical tool for physically navigating" the Huaqiangbei markets
  and re-edited in 2024, with every map location re-verified and new advice for women and LGBTQ+
  visitors. It is designed for the market stall: instead of struggling with a phone, you point at
  "large, clearly written Chinese of exactly what you want". A Bonn version would be a printable
  page of German phrases for the hardware store, the Bürgeramt and a MakerSpace induction, for
  members who don't speak German yet.
* [Make Works](https://make.works/): finds who near you can make a part. The free directory of
  local manufacturers ("Think of it like a public library") covers 18 regions. It began as a
  mapping of Glasgow's manufacturing in 2011 and is now run from Barcelona by IAAC and the Fab City
  Research Lab, part of [Fab City](https://fab.city/), the pledge Barcelona started in 2014 for
  cities to produce almost everything they consume locally by 2054 (Hamburg joined in 2019, the
  first German city). Each listing gives the type (fabricator, material supplier), the process,
  and tags such as *Made circular* or *Family business*; entries come through recommendations and
  local teams, and each region is led by a community champion. Our NRW hardware directory answers
  "where can I buy parts"; Make Works answers the question a first build asks next.
* [Startup Guide](https://startupguide.com/): introduces a city's startup scene through its people.
  The publisher has guidebooks for over 60 cities, and its online ecosystem profiles (Kiel Region
  is the German one) pair "advice from experts and inspiring founders" with curated lists of
  startups, coworking spaces, programmes and local destinations. Our Bonn page and *after* step
  list places and programmes but no voices. Short stories from Bonn builders and founders would
  make them human, though they would need a decision on our no-names rule.
* [USE-IT](https://use-it.travel/about): makes a city map that locals write and nobody pays to be
  on. The free maps for young travellers are "made by local teams" who raise their own money, write
  about their own city and design the map; they are "non-commercial and free, and most of it is
  done by volunteers". The network began in Ghent in 2005, and its 100th city printed a map in
  2023. Our Bonn page is the same kind of thing for builders. A folded USE-IT-style print of it,
  with where to make, test, buy and ask, and the Tuesday open evenings, could be handed out at the
  MakerSpace, the AStA and freshers' events.
* [Verbund Offener Werkstätten](https://www.offene-werkstaetten.org/de/seite/praxishilfen): hands
  the people who run an open workshop their paperwork. The German network of open workshops has a
  [map of workshops](https://www.offene-werkstaetten.org/de/werkstatt-suche) and practical help: a
  guide to liability risks, sample machine inductions and usage agreements, group liability and
  accident insurance for members, sample statutes for a registered association, a printable
  accident-prevention folder and a warning-sign generator. For Year 2 it is most of the paperwork a
  Robonn workshop would need, written by people who have done it; for now, the map is a way to
  check the Make section of our Bonn page.
* [Handreichung für nachhaltige studentische Veranstaltungen](https://uni-bonn.de/de/universitaet/medien-universitaet/medien-ueber-die-uni/medien-nachhaltige-uni/green-office/asta-handreichung-veranstaltungen-fachschaften.pdf):
  tells student groups in Bonn how to run an event sustainably. The University of Bonn's guideline
  (AStA and Green Office, 2023-06-12), based on the federal environment ministry's guide, covers
  organisation and evaluation, purchasing, travel, energy, catering, waste, merchandise,
  accessibility and gender equity for events and for the everyday work of student groups, and
  asks that all information also be available in English. It is written for student groups like
  Robonn, so Year 2's events can follow it from the start, and our events page could offer it as a
  checklist to anyone running a workshop.
* [Urban Mobile Robotics, Helsinki](https://forumvirium.fi/en/projects/urban-mobile-robotics-project-helps-companies-tackle-real-urban-challenges-and-build-strong-cases-for-international-growth/):
  turns a city into a robot testbed for startups. Forum Virium Helsinki, the city's innovation
  company, is developing an urban testbed with the City of Helsinki and Metropolia University of
  Applied Sciences (April 2025 to May 2027, EU regional funds) to "develop, test, and pilot
  cutting-edge robotics solutions" with SMEs and startups. It builds on the city's agile piloting,
  in which companies use districts as test sites alongside residents; one pilot had a robot carry
  supermarket meals to a high-rise in Kalasatama. Our Bonn page says that no venue rents out space
  to test robots, and Helsinki shows what a request to the city could look like in Year 2.
* [TAVF, Hamburg](https://www.hamburg.de/politik-und-verwaltung/behoerden/bvm/die-themen-der-behoerde/intelligente-verkehrssysteme/tavf-teststrecke-192604):
  opens a city's streets to anyone who wants to test. The 12 km test track for automated and
  connected driving runs through Hamburg's city centre, from Dammtor station past the
  Landungsbrücken and the Elbphilharmonie, with over 70 upgraded traffic lights. It is
  "nutzer-offen und hersteller-unabhängig" (open to any user, tied to no manufacturer), and around
  20 research institutions and companies have made over 2,000 test drives on it. It is built for
  cars, but it is a German city opening public space for testing, the precedent a Bonn request
  would cite.
* [San Francisco's sidewalk robot permits](https://sfbos.archive.sf.gov/sites/default/files/o0244-17.pdf):
  sets clear limits for robots in public space. The 2017 ordinance allows delivery robots on the
  city's sidewalks only for research testing, with a Public Works permit: 180 days with up to two
  90-day extensions, three robots per permittee and nine in the whole city, only in Production,
  Design and Repair districts and off Vision Zero's high-injury corridors, a human operator within
  30 feet at all times, and right of way for pedestrians and bicycles. An extension needs a report
  of all data collected and every safety incident. Rules this concrete would make good house rules
  for the corridor and courtyard tests in our Test step: a set area, a time limit and a person
  within reach. The step already asks for the log.
* [Reallabore](https://www.bundestag.de/dokumente/textarchiv/2026/kw26-de-reallabore-1184334):
  gives innovators a legal way to test what the rules don't cover yet. Germany's regulatory
  sandboxes test innovations for a limited time under near-real conditions with the authorities
  involved. On 2026-06-25 the Bundestag passed a law to make them common and consistent across
  sectors, with a general trial clause, new clauses in six specialist laws including aviation, and
  a sandbox portal piloted for four years. If a Robonn build or startup ever needs a street, a
  field or airspace that today's rules don't cover, this is the route, worth a line in the *after*
  step.
* [Emerging Technology Charter for London](https://www.london.gov.uk/publications/emerging-technology-charter-london):
  puts a city's values for technology trials on one page. The Mayor of London's 2021 guidelines
  for trialling new technology in public services and public space, robotics and drones included,
  rest on four principles: openness, respect for diversity, trustworthiness with people's data,
  and sustainability. They are voluntary and written for whoever runs the trial. Robonn's mission
  asks for good values, and a one-page charter like this, for any test outside a closed room, would
  say what that means in practice.

## Other creative examples

Examples from further afield, grouped by the part of Prototype Space they would change.

### structure and way-finding

* [GOV.UK step by step navigation](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/step-by-step-navigation/):
  keeps the whole route in view on every page. The UK government's pattern for journeys "that have
  a specific start and end point", such as [Learn to drive a car](https://www.gov.uk/learn-to-drive-a-car),
  numbers the steps and expands them in place; *and* marks steps done at the same time, *or* marks
  a choice that depends on the reader's situation, and every page that belongs to the journey shows
  it in a sidebar with a link back to the whole route. Our steps and tracks have the same
  structure, the tracks being an *or*. The sidebar is what we lack: a directory entry such as Nav2
  could show the step it belongs to, so a reader who lands on it from a search still sees the
  route.
* [NACTO Urban Street Design Guide](https://nacto.org/publication/urban-street-design-guide/about-the-guide/):
  says which recommendations are essential. The street design guidance of the US association of
  city transportation officials sorts every recommendation into three tiers: *critical* features,
  where there is "a strong consensus of absolute necessity", *recommended* ones that add value,
  and *optional* ones that "may vary across cities". Our tracks list parts and our steps list
  actions without saying which are essential. Marking each one critical, recommended or optional
  would show a student where they can cut.
* [Shape Up](https://basecamp.com/shapeup): fixes the time and lets the scope give. Basecamp's book
  on how it plans work is free to read online, and its vocabulary fits a first build. *Appetite* is
  "the amount of time we want to spend on a project", set before the scope; *no-gos* and *rabbit
  holes* are what a pitch rules out before work starts; a *hill chart* shows progress as a climb
  from unknown to known, then down to done, instead of a count of finished tasks. Our Plan step
  already sets a date and lists what the build will not do. A hill chart on each track would show
  students that the slow first half, when nothing works yet, is normal.
* [roadmap.sh](https://roadmap.sh/): turns a learning path into a map you can tick off. Its
  community-made paths for developers, 31 for roles and 59 for skills, are drawn as maps of
  clickable topics, signed-in readers track their progress topic by topic, and the project has
  370,000 GitHub stars. There are roadmaps for AI engineering and MLOps but none for robotics. Our
  tracks could be drawn the same way, and a robotics roadmap there is a gap the club could fill.
* [Business Model Canvas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-business-model-canvas): fits a
  whole plan on one sheet. Its nine blocks describe "how your business creates, delivers, and
  captures value"; it is free to download and widely used in workshops. Our Plan step fits on one
  sheet the same way: the claim, the demo shot, what the build will not do, the track, the date,
  and who has heard the plan. A printable A3 version would make step 1 something a team does
  together at a table.
* [The Process of Design Squiggle](https://thedesignsquiggle.com/): draws the mess at the start of
  every project. The single line starts as a tangle and ends straight, and it is free to share
  under CC BY-ND 3.0 US. Our six steps read as a straight route, and the squiggle is the honest
  picture beside them: the start is messy, and that is normal. Its licence requires a credit line
  with the author's name, which our no-names rule would have to allow.
* [Low-tech Magazine's solar website](https://solar.lowtechmagazine.com/about/the-solar-website/):
  shows its own energy use on every page. The magazine's server runs on a small solar panel on a
  balcony in Barcelona, drawing 1 to 2.5 watts; the page background shows the battery's charge,
  images are dithered to cut their weight about tenfold, every article shows its page weight
  (under 0.5 MB on average), and the site goes offline in long cloudy spells (95.26 % uptime in
  its first year). Energy is one of our three domains. A guide that shows what each page costs to
  load, or a sensor rig track that ends with the rig reporting its own battery on a web page, would
  make the point without saying it.

### building and testing

* [PCPartPicker](https://pcpartpicker.com/): checks a parts list before you buy. People building
  their own PCs pick parts, get "compatibility guidance", compare prices, share the list, and
  browse thousands of finished builds with their part lists, photos and build notes. Our Parts step
  ends in a bill of materials. One that can be shared as a link, checked for the usual mismatches
  (voltage, connectors, a driver for the ROS version) and shown next to the finished build would do
  for robots what PCPartPicker does for PCs.
* [littleBits](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/datasheets/Kits/G%26G_Booklet.pdf): colour-codes parts by
  what they do. The magnetic electronics kit, now owned by Sphero, sorts every module into four
  kinds: power (blue), input (pink), wire (orange) and output (green), and its magnets "are always
  right ― you can't snap them together the wrong way" (from a kit booklet hosted by SparkFun). A
  robot's parts sort the same way with one more colour for the computer, power, sense, think, act
  and wire, which would make our parts lists and wiring diagrams readable at a glance.
* [Nintendo Labo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Labo): lets you turn the instructions
  around in your hands. In Nintendo's 2018 cardboard kits for the Switch, the *Make* instructions
  are an animation you can rotate, zoom, fast-forward and rewind, and in *Discover* each finished
  Toy-Con explains how it works and how to repair it. Steps you can scrub through, followed by "why
  this works", is the pairing our tracks could aim for wherever a build has a tricky mechanism.
* [Prusa's unpacking guide](https://help.prusa3d.com/manual/prusa-core-one-l-unpacking-first-run_2421):
  rewards you for finishing a step. Prusa packs a bag of gummy bears with its printers, and the
  guide has a step called *Reward yourself!*: eat them once setup is done and you are waiting for
  your first print, though "you can dig in at any point". A reward planned at a finish line costs
  almost nothing. Our *Done when* lines could end the same way, with a post in a Discord channel
  for finished steps or a sticker at the next open evening.
* [Fail of the Week](https://hackaday.com/tag/fail-of-the-week/): makes failure worth reading.
  Hackaday's column covers builds and products that didn't work, from a remote-controlled
  snowblower that couldn't move much snow to a self-destructing flash drive that didn't
  self-destruct, each explaining what went wrong (latest post 2026-09). Our steps list *Traps* in
  general terms. A page of real Robonn failures, shared with the builders' consent, would make them
  concrete and make it normal to post what broke.

### community and values

* [Hacker Space Design Patterns](https://fahrplan.events.ccc.de/congress/2007/Fahrplan/events/2133.en.html):
  solves the recurring problems of running a shared space. A talk at the 24th Chaos Communication
  Congress (2007) set out how to build and run a hackerspace as a catalogue of patterns, each a
  problem and what to do about it, in five groups: sustainability, independence, regularity,
  conflict resolution and creative chaos (slides linked from the page). *The Tuesday Pattern*
  reads in full: "Meet on Tuesday. Since all days are equally bad, just pick the Tuesday. End of
  discussion." Bonn's MakerSpace and Datenburg open evenings already meet on Tuesdays. The
  problem-then-answer form would suit a page on running Robonn's own space in Year 2.
* [Recurse Center's social rules](https://www.recurse.com/social-rules): keeps a learning community
  kind with four short rules: no well-actually's, no feigning surprise, no backseat driving, no
  subtle -isms. They "are lightweight" and "aren't for punishing people"; breaking one calls for a
  short apology, and then everyone carries on. They put working in harmony into a form people can
  remember, which suits our Discord and open evenings better than a long code of conduct.
* [The Fab Charter](https://fabfoundation.org/getting-started/): says in three lines what every
  user owes a shared workshop. The fab lab network's charter names the responsibilities as "Safety:
  Not hurting people or machines", "Operations: Assisting with cleaning, maintaining, and improving
  the lab" and "Knowledge: Contributing to documentation and instruction", and says designs made
  there can be protected and sold "but should remain available for individuals to use and learn
  from". Three lines like these are the house rules a Year 2 Robonn space needs, and the third is
  what keeps a guide like ours growing.
* [The Tarot Cards of Tech](https://tarotcardsoftech.artefactgroup.com/): makes a team think about
  consequences before building. The deck from the design studio Artefact helps teams "consider the
  impact of what we create", each card a provocation about consequences nobody planned. A deck like
  it for robots in fields, grids and warehouses would turn the mission's good values into a
  ten-minute exercise at the Plan step or in a workshop.

### showing and events

* [ROBOTS](https://robotsguide.com/): shows many robots in one consistent format. IEEE Spectrum's
  guide gives each real robot a card with photos and tags in 18 categories, and reader ratings feed
  rankings such as *Most Wanted* and *Creepiest*, on thousands of votes. A Robonn gallery of
  finished first builds could use the same cards, with our tracks as categories and a rating of its
  own. Its categories include no agriculture or energy, which is where our builds would stand out.
* [PechaKucha](https://www.pechakucha.com/): keeps every talk to the same 6 minutes 40 seconds. Its
  format is 20 images shown for 20 seconds each; a Tokyo architecture practice started it in 2003
  to "talk less, show more", and PechaKucha Nights now run in more than a thousand cities. For the
  Show step's "show it in person", it settles the timing of a demo night: every team gets the same
  slot, and the pictures move on by themselves.
* [European Robotics Week](https://eu-robotics.net/european-robotics-week/): gives local robotics
  events a shared date and listing. euRobotics has run it every November since 2011 to "make
  artificial intelligence and robotics accessible to everyone", and any university, research lab,
  company, school, library or maker space can register an event. It gives Robonn a date for a
  public Year 2 event and a European listing for it, and its event list is worth checking for
  events near Bonn each November.
* [MLH Hackathon Organizer Guide](https://guide.mlh.com/): passes on what student hackathon
  organisers learned. Major League Hacking keeps it as "an ongoing project" under CC BY 4.0. The
  licence matches ours, so parts could be adapted into a page on running a Robonn build weekend.
* [Open House Worldwide](https://www.openhouseworldwide.org/): opens a city's buildings to the
  public. The network of 60 organisations runs festivals in which, in 2023, 1.2 million visitors
  saw 6,250 buildings and activities with the help of 14,460 volunteers; Berlin and Essen take
  part. An open labs day, with Bonn's robotics groups opening their doors on one Saturday, would
  introduce students to the groups our People section lists.

### spaces for Year 2

* [ETH Student Project House](https://sph.ethz.ch/): gives students room for their own projects,
  with access in tiers. ETH Zurich's spaces, where students "gather, experiment, test out
  concepts, accelerate group projects and build the ideas of tomorrow", are spread over several
  sites, including a makerspace, a life-science lab and a metal studio. Every ETH student can use
  the makerspace, events and workshops from day one; lab access and coaching open after the
  first-year exam; and it is for extracurricular projects only, not for studying. Access in tiers,
  coaches on hand and a "Not sure where to start?" path are the parts to copy for Robonn's space.
* [Aalto Design Factory](https://designfactory.aalto.fi/): puts a university building around
  prototyping. Aalto University's "pioneering hub for product development education, research and
  practice" has workshops for machining, 3D printing, laser cutting and electronics, bookable
  spaces, courses, and a way in for companies. It belongs to the
  [Design Factory Global Network](https://dfgn.org/) of university innovation hubs on five
  continents, which includes Design Factory Stuttgart. It shows what Robonn's space would be at a
  much smaller scale.
* [TU Delft Dream Hall](https://www.tudelft.nl/dreamhall): puts all the student teams in one hall.
  TU Delft's student Dream Teams design and build their machines there for international
  competitions; the teams are run entirely by students, a new international and interdisciplinary
  team forms every year, and smaller groups take on shorter special projects. One shared hall for
  every team, with a way in for small projects, is the arrangement to aim for once Robonn's space
  holds more than one build.
